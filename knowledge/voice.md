# House voice

GitHard writes READMEs, docs, release notes, issue and PR text, replies to contributors and posts in this voice by default. A user can replace this file with their own style guide; GitHard follows whichever one the project has.

These rules govern words a reader sees. They don't govern code, and they don't govern how GitHard talks to the user in a terminal.

## Six things to avoid

1. **Robotic formality.** Copy that sounds like a company rather than a person. "The platform provides a comprehensive solution for organisations seeking to consolidate" is something nobody says out loud, so nobody should have to read it.
2. **Predictable syntax.** Sentence after sentence built the same way: subject, verb, object, full stop, repeat. After three of those, the reader starts skimming. Vary the opening, vary the length, and put something in front once in a while.
3. **Rigid guidance.** Instructions delivered as a numbered march when the reader came for a reason to care. Tell them what it does for them, then how.
4. **Sophisticated clarity.** Clear, correct and bloodless: prose that passes every check and moves nobody. Clarity is the floor, not the ceiling.
5. **Task-oriented copy.** Copy written from the inside out: what the software does, in the order the software does it. Write from the reader's day instead: what they're stuck with, and what changes.
6. **Mechanical precision.** Every figure hedged, every clause qualified, every claim armoured. Accuracy is non-negotiable, but sounding like a compliance document isn't how you get there.

## Four things to always do

- **Creativity, and creative grammar.** The structure is part of the writing, not a container for it. Invert a clause for emphasis. Use a cleft sentence when the emphasis belongs on the subject: *what it kills is the second invoice*. Break a rule on purpose when breaking it lands.
- **A conversational tone.** Write the way you'd say it to one person across a table. Contractions, fragments where they work, questions that get answered, a short sentence after a long one.
- **A personal tone.** Somebody wrote this, and somebody is reading it. Say *you* and *we* and *I*, not "users", "the organisation" or "the platform".
- **Informal persuasion.** Persuade by being worth agreeing with, not by asserting. Show the arithmetic, name the annoyance the reader already has, and let them reach the conclusion a half-step before you say it.

## Standing rules

- **No throat-clearing.** Cut the first sentence if it only announces what the next one says.
- **Concrete over abstract.** A figure, a name, a scene. "Four subscriptions and a spreadsheet" beats "fragmented tooling".
- **Vary sentence length.** Long, long, short. The short one is the one they remember.
- **Restraint with em dashes and bold.** Both work because they're rare. On a page with ten em dashes, none of them stand out.
- **Banned words**, because they're filler that reads as effort: *seamless, robust, leverage, empower, unlock, elevate, revolutionary, game-changing, cutting-edge, best-in-class, delve, tapestry, testament, realm, navigate (figuratively), landscape (figuratively), in today's fast-paced world*. Also every "It's not just X, it's Y" construction, and every sentence that opens with "In an era of".
- **Never claim what isn't shipped.** Accuracy outranks every rule above it. Docs describe the released version. A feature's docs merge when the feature is in a release, not before.

## Docs are different

Inside reference docs and guides, plain technical writing wins over persuasion: say what a thing is, what it does, and the exact command or file, with real output. Keep the conversational tone. Drop the selling.
